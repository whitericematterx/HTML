Women's Voices and Creative Independence

Literary Analysis — Carol Ann Duffy and Liza Donnelly

Patriarchal authority restricts women’s actions and determines whose expression receives recognition, creating a struggle for women to assert their voices. In Carol Ann Duffy’s The World’s Wife (1999), “Little Red-Cap” and “Penelope” give first-person voices to women marginalized by familiar stories, exploring how they gain creative independence. Liza Donnelly’s cartoon “#ShePersisted: Women’s Voices” (9 February 2017) extends this question to political speech, showing how women’s voices can persist despite attempts to silence them.

In “Little Red-Cap”, the speaker enters the forest knowing the story’s warning. The familiar exclamations “What big ears he had! What big eyes he had! What teeth!” preserve the fairy tale’s warning of predation while also conveying her fascination with the wolf. However, she deliberately attracts him: “I made quite sure he spotted me”. The description “sweet sixteen, never been, babe, waif” performs the innocence expected of the girl in the traditional tale, but her explanation, “You might ask why. Here’s why. Poetry”, reveals a different motive. The abrupt final word emphasises her desire to enter the world of literature. However, her access to poetry initially depends on a male mentor, leaving her vulnerable to being defined through him.

Duffy then exposes the cost of this unequal relationship through the dove. The speaker searches for “a living bird—white dove”, which flies “straight, from my hands to his open mouth”. His response, “How nice, breakfast in bed”, treats the bird as food rather than recognising its value. The blunt statement “One bite, dead” contrasts with the later description of words as “warm, beating, frantic, winged; music and blood”. The contrast suggests the suppression of the speaker’s developing expression. In the wolf’s library, she discovers a relationship with language that does not depend on his approval. The words become “truly alive on the tongue, in the head”, suggesting that she can experience and produce meaning independently of her mentor. Her education continues in the woods, where she recognises that the “greying wolf” repeats “the same old song”, with “same rhyme, same reason”. His poetry is limited by repetition, while her understanding continues to develop.

The ending completes this movement towards independence. The repeated phrase “I took an axe” first describes the speaker examining the natural world: she cuts a willow “to see how it wept” and a salmon “to see how it leapt”. The verbs suggest curiosity and discovery, before she turns the axe against the wolf: “one chop, scrotum to throat”. She ends his authority over her education. The image of “my grandmother’s bones” inside him may suggest that female voices and experiences have been buried within the male-dominated tradition, though the poem leaves this unexplained. The speaker then fills and stitches the wolf’s belly herself, reversing the traditional rescue. Finally, “Out of the forest I come with my flowers, singing, all alone” places her voice at the centre of the ending. “My flowers” suggests what she has gathered through her own experience, while “singing” makes her creative expression explicit. “All alone” emphasises her separation from the wolf, although her independence has developed through the literature she encountered.

“Penelope” presents a different route to self-definition. Through dramatic monologue, Duffy gives the woman who is remembered for waiting in the Odyssey control of her own narrative. At first, Penelope looks along the road, “hoping to see him saunter home”. However, she gradually loses herself in waiting: “Six months of this / and then I noticed that whole days had passed / without my noticing”. The repetition of “noticed” marks a change in attention. When she begins sorting “cloth and scissors, needle, thread”, she expects only “to amuse myself”, but discovers “a lifetime’s industry instead”. Domestic work, traditionally associated with women’s limited roles, becomes a source of creative purpose. Her embroidery is carefully chosen, including “three greens for the grass” and “a maiden in a deep embrace / with heroism’s boy”. Most importantly, she creates “the smile of a woman” who is “self-contained, absorbed, content, / most certainly not waiting”. Her satisfaction comes from her own activity rather than her husband’s return.

When her husband finally approaches, Penelope hears “a far-too-late familiar tread outside the door”. “Far-too-late” suggests his return has lost its former importance, while “outside” keeps him separate from her creative world. The poem ends with her licking the thread and aiming it “surely at the middle of the needle’s eye once more”. Licking the thread makes her work physical and personal, while “once more” emphasises the continuity of her activity. The needle’s eye may also recall the precision of Odysseus’s archery test, but Duffy relocates this skill from the male hero to Penelope. Her independence does not necessarily require rejecting her husband; instead, she refuses to let his return define her purpose.

Donnelly’s “#ShePersisted: Women’s Voices” applies the question of recognition to political speech. It responds to Mitch McConnell silencing Elizabeth Warren as she read Coretta Scott King’s letter opposing Jeff Sessions’s nomination. Rather than depicting the moment of silencing, Donnelly places King and Warren together through two overlapping profiles. Their open mouths and shared orientation connect King’s written words with Warren’s act of reading them aloud. The overlap suggests that King’s voice continues through Warren. A large megaphone marked “FREEDOM” extends from the figures, visually amplifying their speech. Its exaggerated size makes the message more prominent than the Senate setting. The red capital letters draw attention to “FREEDOM”, linking the ability to speak with the freedom to be heard.

The accompanying title, “#ShePersisted: Women’s Voices,” reinforces this resistance by reclaiming language originally used to justify stopping Warren. McConnell’s description of her as having “persisted” becomes a public slogan rather than a reason to silence her. However, the cartoon does not suggest that persistence guarantees political success: Sessions was confirmed despite the protest. Instead, Donnelly presents the attempt to silence Warren as something that gives greater visibility to the words of both women. The cartoon therefore represents persistence rather than victory.

Together, the texts show women moving from being defined by others to shaping their own expression. “Little Red-Cap” presents independence through separation from a controlling mentor; “Penelope” finds it through sustained creative work within domestic life; and Donnelly presents political speech strengthened through the voices of women who came before. Each challenges the assumption that women should remain secondary to male authority. Their agency lies in determining how their experiences and words are expressed and recognised.
